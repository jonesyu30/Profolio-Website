I finished *The Martian* on 23 July 2026. 

Honestly, Mark Watney’s personality feels like Technoblade and Dream combined into a single NASA astronaut. He has top-tier problem-solving skills under absolute pressure, paired with a deadpan, sarcastic humor that keeps him sane in dire situations. He’s chilling at the literal end of the world, and honestly, we all need a bit of that mindset.

The book is semi-long, but it’s an surprisingly smooth read. Because there’s biology, chemistry, physics, and orbital mechanics in almost every single chapter, I’m not sure if people without a scientific background would love it as much—but if you enjoy science, it’s a masterpiece.

---

## Three Subtle Ways This Book Changed Me

### 1. The Courage to "Blow Up Your Ship"
The sheer act of rebellion against a completely hopeless situation is wildly motivating. When all odds are stacked against you, there is *always* something more you can do—it just depends on how bad you want it. 

When you’re backed into a corner, you can either accept failure or deliberately blow up your airlock to survive. These last-resort options exist in real life, too. You just need the smarts to think of them and the courage to execute them. In the real world, a lot of people will call your decisions crazy because they can't see the solution themselves. All you need is faith in your knowledge, physics, and the courage to act.

### 2. "One Thing at a Time"
If I remember only one quote from this book, it’s this one:

> *"One thing at a time."*

This line instantly lowers your stress. Instead of mixing all your problems into a giant bowl of "stressful soup," you just pick one task, focus on it, and finish it before moving to the next. Reading this actually helped me finally understand the core philosophy behind the productivity book *The ONE Thing*.

### 3. Appreciating the Smallest Wins
When you're stuck alone on a dead planet, everything becomes worth celebrating: planting potatoes, successfully connecting to NASA, talking to another human being, or simply surviving another day. You get to live one more day, so you might as well be happy about it. Finishing this book genuinely felt like a mini rebirth.

## Direct Astronauts vs. Earth Politics

One thing I loved was the stark contrast between the crew and the people on Earth. 

The astronauts tell the raw truth without hiding anything—they are pure-minded, focused solely on the mission. When they tell Watney directly about their trajectory issues, I was initially shocked because I assumed they would beat around the bush (which is probably a psychological defense mechanism most of us use on Earth). 

Meanwhile, the executives back home are stressing over public image, political fallout, and funding—all the extra noise I hate worrying about.

I also loved how the disasters slowly built up. Just like in *Final Destination*, the failures weren't out of nowhere—they were planted years ago, like a manufacturing flaw in an airlock or sand settling over thousands of years. Death keeps coming for Watney and fails again and again. Or, looking at it from the other side, human ingenuity wins again and again. 

I guess that’s what life is really about.

## Favorite Quotes & Realizations

> *"Right now, they're suffering the worst pain anyone can endure. I'd give anything just to let them know I'm still alive. I'll just have to survive to make up for it."*

Watney is never a selfish guy. He constantly emphasizes that his crewmates did what they had to do based on bad luck, and none of it was their fault.

> *"Well. Beggars can't be choosers."*

When you have nothing left, you use what you've got. Simple as that.

> *"I suppose I'll think of something. Or die."*

This line basically summarizes the entire book: improvising ways to accomplish things no one at NASA foresaw.

> *"Mark, you sneaky, clever, son of a bitch."*

When NASA slowly realizes what Mark is planning on the surface, the reveal is so satisfying that I almost screamed out loud while reading. I rarely get that excited while reading fiction, but the anticipation and payoff were executed flawlessly.

> *"So instead, I'll do something that wouldn't work at all on Earth: throw them outside."*

Pure Technoblade energy—taking complex NASA engineering and oversimplifying it so a kindergartener could understand it.

> *"'The supplies wouldn't be the only source of food,' she said. He widened his eyes. 'Oh.. oh my god...'"*

I caught on to what she meant instantly (probably because I’ve been reading about the *Miracle of the Andes* disaster recently). It shows how insanely high the stakes were if the supply run failed.

> *"Every human being has a basic instinct to help each other out. It might not seem that way sometimes, but it's true."*

I'm still a bit cynical about this—I think humans are inherently selfish until proven otherwise. But watching the entire world pull together to save one person on Mars makes you want to believe it.
