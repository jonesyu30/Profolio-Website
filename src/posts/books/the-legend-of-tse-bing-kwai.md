I finished reading *謝炳奎傳奇* (*The Legend of Tse Bing-kwai*) on 1 September 2026.

This was a unique reading experience—my first time picking up a biography of someone so close to my own life, yet historically so far away. Because our paths overlap in a very specific setting, reading the early chapters felt less like reading someone else's biography and more like unlocking my own memories. When he described his scouting experiences, I could see every detail in my mind through my own background, even if I lacked the rich vocabulary he used to describe it so beautifully.

I'll be returning this copy to the Scout room soon, but a few core takeaways stuck with me.

---

## 1. Navigating Biographies and Survivorship Bias

The book frames Tse as someone who dedicated his entire life to scouting. It’s an inspiring narrative, but you have to keep a critical eye on transcription bias. The authors and interviewers clearly admired him, so certain stories are naturally framed in the best possible light.

The nostalgic portrayal of historical scouting raised a few questions for me:

**The "Golden Age" Fallacy:** Scouting in the past is painted as incredibly chill, bright, and pure. While the movement was much smaller back then, the members seemed passionately committed. 

**Survivorship Bias:** The people who write or talk about scouting decades later are naturally the ones who were most dedicated to it. 

**Stepping Up:** Interesting counter-details pop up throughout the text. Multiple times, others passed on leadership positions with questionable excuses, leaving Tse to take on the responsibility himself. It shows that even back then, true commitment was rare.

---

## 2. History Beyond Words

The chapters covering the war period were the hardest to get through. Not because the writing was poor, but because of the sheer weight of the history:

> Reading about the war felt painful and suffocating. It’s a strange feeling knowing that no matter how many books you read, you will only ever get a cheap, artificial knock-off of what that generation actually lived through.

Knowing that these events happened in the exact same geographic space makes it feel uncomfortably close, yet emotionally distant. 

---

## 3. "Only Scouts Can Understand Scouts"

Tse comes across as a genuinely great person—well-connected, successful, and deeply respected. If I were fully dedicated to the scouting movement as my life's path, I would probably idolize him. But as a participant looking from a slight distance, I view him simply as a towering role model for future leaders.

Scouting leaves you with one lasting realization:

> *"Only scouts can understand scouts."*

People who have never been part of it simply won't get what standard activities, campfires, or traditions mean to those inside it. 

If you're stepping into leadership within the movement, this book serves as a solid historical mirror. It's an insightful, grounding read before it goes back onto the shelf in the Scout room.
