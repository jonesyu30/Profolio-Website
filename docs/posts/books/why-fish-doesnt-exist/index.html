Why Fish Don't Exist

By Lulu Miller

Table of Contents

    I finished Why Fish Don't Exist on 8 July 2026.

    This is easily the best-written book I’ve read so far. The level of English prose, the romantic descriptions, and the sheer storytelling are far above anything else I’ve picked up. If it weren't for my Kobo's built-in dictionary, I would have given up on the vocabulary weeks ago.

    It’s part biography of taxonomist David Starr Jordan, part memoir, and part existential investigation. I needed a book like this ever since taking HKDSE Biology. Back then, everything around me started to feel cold, preset, and mechanical—just laws of chemistry, physics, and math, with no real room for human meaning. Reading The Selfish Gene back in the day actually made that feeling worse. But Lulu Miller's book felt strangely healing.

    1. Study Nature, Not Books (And Don't Trust Either Blindly)

    "No, if you were satisfied with the beliefs of the day, Agassiz worried, it kept you stunted, stymied, sick. The way out, the way to enlightenment, was to keep looking closer, longer, at the pebbles and petals and pelts of this world."

    There’s a constant warning throughout the book: if you blindly accept the current theories or established categories of your time, you stay stunted and sick.

    Books are a great escape (and as a lazy person, I love escaping into them), but thoughts and human theories can always be wrong. You have to look at the world itself. Categories like genus, family, order, or class aren't real—nature doesn't draw hard lines. The exact same thing applies to fields like psychology (which I'm quickly realizing I have a love-hate relationship with); human categories are just arbitrary curtains drawn over a chaotic reality.

    2. The Illusion of the Hierarchy: There Is No Ladder

    For a long time, I instinctively thought humans were superior—the fastest, the smartest, the ultimate creation at the top of the food chain. But science proves otherwise:

    "We're not the fastest or the strongest or the most prolific. We're not the only ones that mate for life, that show altruism, use tools, language... This was what Darwin was trying so hard to get his readers to see. There is no ladder."

    There is no natural ladder. We aren't above anything, and nothing is below us.

    Homogeneity is a death sentence; variation is the only thing keeping species alive. Realizing this actually made my existential anxiety fade a bit. No matter how weird, extreme, or "unsuccessful" your life might seem by human standards, you exist as part of the endless genetic and behavioral diversity of humanity. You aren't "failing" a climb up a non-existent ladder.

    3. Believing in Good When Nature Doesn't Care

    One of the most devastating themes in the book is watching David Starr Jordan have thirty years of his life’s work—thousands of preserved fish specimens—destroyed in the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. And what does he do? He takes a needle and thread and literally sews the name tags directly into the flesh of the surviving fish.

    "Maybe it is the quiet work of believing in Good. Of believing in a warmth, which you know does not exist in the stars, to exist in the hearts of fellow humans. Maybe it is something like trust."

    Nature is completely indifferent to us. Its physical laws are immutable, and trying to fight them is like "wielding a club of air." Yet, human persistence—stubbornly believing in order, warmth, and meaning even when the universe gives you zero evidence for it—is both terrifying and breathtaking.

    Beautiful Lines That Stuck With Me

    "The two would grow close that summer, exploring the shores of Penikese by moonlight, slipping their ankles into the black waves to ignite firework displays of twinkling green bioluminescence."

    The descriptions in this book are unreal. It weaves science with imagery that feels deeply romantic, detailed, and dreamy.

    "But she loved David; she loved the way he loved the world."

    If anyone ever asks, "What do you love about me?"—this is the most perfect, elegant answer in existence.

    "Sciosophy. The unfortunate fusion of science with philosophy."

    A sobering reminder of what happens when overconfident people use bad science to justify eugenics and decide who is "fit" to exist. It happens when people try to force their hopeful fantasies into hard facts.

    The Verdict

    We are a speck of a speck in an indifferent universe. Trying to make sense of whether we matter is like an ouroboros eating its own tail—a complete contradiction. But as Miller demonstrates, dismantling our neat, comfortable categories (like realizing "fish" don't scientifically exist as a valid single group) doesn't ruin the world. It strips away a man-made curtain and lets you see the real, messy universe behind it.

    A masterpiece of a book. Heavy, poetic, and absolutely worth the dictionary lookups.
