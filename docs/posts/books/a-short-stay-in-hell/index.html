A Short Stay in Hell

    A Short Stay in Hell: What Powers of Years Taught Me About Loneliness

    I just finished my very first fiction book on my new Kobo—A Short Stay in Hell by Steven L. Peck. It’s also the first book I’ve managed to finish in a single day.

    The title is almost funny when you read it. On Earth, we are talking about powers and powers of years—falling for light years just to reach the bottom of a library. It isn't short or small by any human measure. But compared to eternity? It’s just a drop in the ocean.

    As a math student, I couldn't stop thinking about the difference between the finite and the infinite. There’s a line in the book that says finite doesn't mean much if you can't tell any practical difference between it and infinite. But in math, the difference changes everything:

    $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\text{A Very Large Number}}{x} = 0$$

    No natural number, no matter how astronomically huge, is large compared to infinity. If hell were truly infinite suffering, what happens in this library doesn't even come close. It really is just a "short stay."

    1. On Love and Running Out of Words

    What got to me the most were the breakups in the story. Love simply wasn't designed to last for billions of years. Eventually, you just run out of things to say to each other.

    It made me realize there are two ways things end:

    • The Hard Break: It’s painful, sharp, and leaves a scar. But it holds value. It leaves the most vivid, memorable mark behind.
    • The Slow Break: It happens after all the value has been drained dry. Nothing remains—no regrets, but no vision left either. Just a quiet drift into nothingness.

    2. Using My Imagination Again

    I haven’t used my imagination to read in a very long time. With self-help or non-fiction books, you just absorb information. But with this, I found my focus drifting unless I actively tried to build the scene in my head—trying to make it feel like a movie.

    At first, I worried my mental picture was "biased" or wrong. Then I realized: that’s literally what reading is. Everyone brings their own view of the world to the page. The exact same text will construct a completely different hell for every person who reads it.

    3. "THE Feeling"

    If I had to describe the core atmosphere of the book, it’s a terrifying mix of strict order and total mental breakdown.

    The books are neatly lined up on endless shelves. The objective is completely straightforward. Everything seems organized, yet your mind has already collapsed a hundred times over. You skip imagining the physical pain because who wants to self-impose that? But you can't escape the mental weight.

    It’s the absolute feeling of helplessness. Not a dramatic, panicked helplessness—just the quiet fact that no one is coming to save you. No one around you can save you. And no one can stay by your side for that long.

    The further you search, the fewer people you encounter. To keep looking for your book, you have to keep moving forward alone. Human determination just wasn't engineered for infinite, repetitive tasks in total isolation.

    The Verdict

    In the end, bad people can turn any place into hell, and the right people can make a wasteland feel tolerable. But when you are stripped of time, rules, and human connection, you're left with a terrifying truth:

    It is completely hopeless, yet giving up isn't even an option.

    It’s a short book—only four chapters—but it stays with you. If you’ve been stuck in a reading slump or just want something that will quietly break your brain for a few hours, pick it up. Just don't expect to look at a library the same way again.
