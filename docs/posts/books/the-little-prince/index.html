The Little Prince

By Antoine de Saint-Exupéry

I finished reading The Little Prince on 3 October 2026.

The ending was completely unexpected, and I’ll do my best not to spoil it here.

The prose is poetic without feeling like an encrypted code—it’s entirely readable, viewing the world through the soft lens of a child filled with stars, distant planets, and roses. There are endless metaphors that I kept trying to decode, but eventually I realized that analyzing every piece wasn't the point. It was enough to just grasp the underlying mood.

Reading this book created a strange, dreamy state of mind. My usual logical system just powered down, making everything feel heavy, quiet, and slow. It makes you feel a bit powerless, but it’s a rare experience to simply turn off your analytical brain and let the feelings take over.

Here are the central reflections and quotes that stuck with me.

1. What Grown-ups Measure vs. Invisible Beauty

The book constantly contrasts how children see the world with how adults reduce everything to numbers and utility:

"If you were to say to grown-ups: 'I saw a beautiful house made of rosy brick, with geraniums in the windows and doves on the roof,' they would not be able to get any idea of that house at all. You would have to say to them: 'I saw a house that cost $20,000.' Then they would exclaim: 'Oh, what a pretty house that is!'"

Grown-ups obsess over price tags and consequence. They swell up with pride just saying "I am busy with matters of consequence!" even when those matters don't bring real joy.

Instead, the book pushes a very different idea: the most meaningful things in life are invisible.

"The house, the stars, the desert—what gives them their beauty is something that is invisible... The eyes are blind. One must look with the heart."

Real beauty isn't something you quantify with your eyes or your wallet; it's something you perceive internally.

2. The Complexities of the Rose & Reasonable Demands

At first, the rose felt like the villain of the story—vain, manipulative, gaslighting the prince, and clinging to him only when he tried to leave. But as the narrative unfolds, her defense mechanisms make a strange kind of sense.

When the prince meets a king on another planet, he encounters a surprisingly grounded lesson on authority:

"If I ordered a general to change himself into a sea bird, and if the general did not obey me, that would not be the fault of the general. It would be my fault."

A leader can only demand what someone is capable of delivering. If a command is absurd, the failure lies with the person issuing it, not the person who disobeys.

3. "The Time You Have Wasted for Your Rose"

The fox delivers what is easily the core thesis of the book regarding human relationships:

"Other steps send me hurrying back underneath the ground. Yours will call me, like music, out of my burrow... It is the time you have wasted for your rose that makes your rose so important."

This is the ultimate answer to that age-old relationship question: "What if someone more beautiful or talented comes along?"

It doesn't matter if there are five thousand identical roses in a single garden. The rose on your planet is singular because of the time, effort, and shared history you poured into it. You use the word "wasted" because that time can never be retrieved—which is precisely what makes the bond irreplaceable.

"Men have forgotten this truth, but you must not forget it. You become responsible, forever, for what you have tamed."

You can't just step into someone’s life, build a connection, consume their time, and walk away. Taming something means accepting a quiet, permanent responsibility for that bond.

Loyalty and Softness

"What moves me so deeply, about this little prince who is sleeping here, is his loyalty to a flower..."

Loyalty doesn't require grand, expensive gestures like buying mansions or pouring money into someone. Sometimes, the most beautiful thing you can offer another person is simply the steadfast choice to stay.

Whether you've watched the animated movie or picked up the original text, The Little Prince hits a very specific chord. It forces you to pause, set aside the relentless drive to be productive, and remember how to look at the world through your heart instead of your eyes.
